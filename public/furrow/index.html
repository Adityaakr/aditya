The problem

You buy GPU-hours and sell tokens. The rate between them keeps moving.

A GPU serves a certain number of tokens per second. That number changes when your serving software updates, when prompts get longer, when your users arrive at the same hour. So the same traffic costs a different number of GPU-hours from one month to the next.

The two halves of the work moved in opposite directions

Serving a request has two parts. Reading the prompt is prefill, limited by raw compute. Writing the answer is decode, limited by memory bandwidth. Over nine monthly benchmark snapshots the decode yield of gpt-oss-120b on H100 rose 84 percent while the prefill yield fell 12 percent. A workload that is mostly prompt got no cheaper; one that is mostly answers nearly halved in cost.

tokens per second per GPU at a floor of 100 tokens per second per user, rebased to October 2025. Expected progress per month: prefill -1.6 percent, decode +7.6 percent; the shocks around those trends have standard deviations of 7.8 and 15.0 percent.

The usual answer is to watch the GPU price. The price is one of four things that move the bill, and for most workloads it is not the biggest.

What Furrow does

It plays out twenty thousand futures, then names what moved the bill.

Prices, demand, serving efficiency and your provider's rate all move at once, calibrated to public benchmarks, index history and traffic traces. Furrow then asks, path by path, which input carried the variance. The method is a Shapley decomposition, the same idea used to split a shared bill fairly.

GPU price, 37 percent. What the market charges per GPU-hour. Reservations or futures can address it.
Your demand, 44 percent. How many tokens your users actually ask for. Reservations cover part of it; forecasting is the lever.
Serving yield, 15 percent. How many tokens each GPU-hour produces. Nothing on the market addresses it.
Provider basis, 6 percent. The gap between your provider's rate and the index. Only a contract on your own provider's price would.

The answer depends on the workload

Run the same engine on five example workloads and the picture changes completely. A coding assistant with short answers is almost all demand risk. A batch pipeline with no latency floor is mostly price. A reasoning model on new hardware is dominated by its own serving yield, because its benchmarks are still moving fast.

What a price hedge can and cannot do

A perfect price hedge removes the price share, and nothing else.

CME plans to list futures on H100 and B200 rental indices, one contract per GPU-month. Furrow sizes a hypothetical strip three ways and measures how much of the variance of your six-month cost each would remove. For the chat example the best rule reaches about 40 percent, close to the price share above. That is the ceiling, and it holds only if the index the contract settles on tracks the one you pay.

The honest number is a range

Every input the simulation cannot observe is a scenario. Move one at a time and the six-month hedge effectiveness for the chat example runs from 18 to 64 percent. Furrow prints the range on the same page as the point estimate.

minimum-variance hedge effectiveness of six-month cost, one input moved at a time with the others at the base scenario; the dashed line is the base case

Two things move it most: how volatile the GPU price turns out to be, and how far the settlement index drifts from the price you actually pay. Neither is known yet. Until the contracts trade, every one of these numbers is labelled a proxy-index simulation.

Why the cost per million tokens is different

Most people convert tokens to dollars with one benchmark figure, usually the 1k-in, 1k-out configuration. A chat workload is 94 percent prompt tokens, and prompt tokens are the cheap half. Pricing the mix with the single benchmark overstates cost by 59 percent. Furrow prices prefill and decode separately, then applies your rate and the utilization your demand shape allows.

The seven pages

  1. The answer: four numbers and one paragraph on where your risk comes from.
  2. Your workload as the model sees it, every input with its status.
  3. Your serving yield and how it has moved in public benchmarks.
  4. Where your cost risk comes from, and what addresses each part.
  5. Procurement policies compared: on demand, reserve for peak, reserve the baseline.
  6. The hedge module: what GPU rental futures could and could not remove, labelled a proxy-index simulation.
  7. Sensitivity, data provenance and the scope of what the report claims.

What goes in

About ten things you already know.

The intake is one short file. Nothing in it is a forecast you have to defend; growth and forecast error have sensible defaults by workload shape, and every default is labelled assumed in the report until you replace it.

  • Trafficmean prompt and generated tokens per request, requests per month, monthly growth
  • Service levelthe latency floor per user: none, 50 or 100 tokens per second
  • Model and hardwarean InferenceX model name or your own benchmark run; H100, H200 or B200
  • Demand shapeconversation, code, flat, or your own peak, p95 and trough ratios
  • Contracton demand, reserved or mixed; your rate, fleet and months remaining; a reservation quote if you have one
  • Scenariosindex volatility, drift, basis volatility, risk premium and settlement correlation, each from a fixed grid so reports stay comparable

Who it is for

The person who owns the compute line.

Companies renting roughly fifty to five thousand GPUs to serve open-weight models, and the people who plan and pay for that capacity.

  • Inference providers"How many GPUs will we need next quarter, and what should we commit to?"The GPU-hour forecast, the reserve-versus-on-demand comparison and the true cost per million tokens for your mix, so pricing and procurement use the same number.
  • AI product companies"We signed a reserved contract last year. Is it the right size?"Enter your fleet, rate and months remaining, and your current contract appears in the policy table beside the alternatives, with idle capacity and overflow made explicit.
  • Finance and procurement"Someone proposed hedging our GPU spend with the new futures. Should we care?"How much of your variance is price at all, and what a hypothetical position could not remove. Furrow sizes; it does not recommend.
  • Researchers"Is serving-yield risk real, and how big?"A public package reproduces the paper, publishes the yield time series as a dataset, and pre-registers the first months of futures trading.

Four moments when it matters

  • Contract renewalWhat fleet the always-on part of your demand justifies, what idle capacity a peak-sized fleet carries, and how the ranking moves with the discount you are actually quoted.
  • Capacity planningA quarterly plan that carries expected efficiency progress and a forecast error you chose, instead of a spreadsheet conversion at today's throughput.
  • Per-token pricingThe cost of a million tokens of your specific mix, prefill and decode priced separately, with the single-benchmark figure shown beside it.
  • "Should we hedge?"The share of variance a price hedge can reach, the sizing rule that avoids compounding your own yield changes, and the range across scenarios.

How every number is tagged

One vocabulary runs through the results file, the report and the screen. A reader can tell at a glance which numbers were measured and which were chosen.

  • observedMeasured inputs: today's index price, your rate, your token counts, a reservation quote.
  • derivedComputed from observed inputs by a stated rule: yields, GPU-hours, cost per million tokens.
  • assumedA default standing in for a number you did not give: the reserved discount, the forecast error, borrowed yield dynamics.
  • scenarioA chosen grid value: index volatility, drift, basis volatility, risk premium, settlement correlation.
  • simulatedA Monte Carlo output under the named scenario: hedge effectiveness, policy costs, variance shares.

What it is not

A simulation under stated scenarios, not a forecast.

  • Not adviceIt sizes a hypothetical futures position and says what it cannot remove. It never recommends taking one.
  • Not a trading toolNo execution, no custody, no live market feed. The futures settle on an index Furrow has no history for yet, and every page that mentions them says so.
  • Not a serviceNo login and no server anyone else can reach. One operator, one customer, one report.
  • Not your stackYield dynamics come from public benchmarks for two models today. For anything else you bring a benchmark run, and the borrowed dynamics are labelled assumed.

The research

Built on a paper you can rerun to the last digit.

TokenYield Working Paper 1, "Tokens to Exposure: How Serving-Yield Risk Limits GPU-Futures Hedges", introduces token yield risk: the uncertainty in turning tokens delivered at a latency target into billable GPU-hours. Furrow wraps its engine without changing a formula.

  • EngineOne command regenerates every figure, table and number of the paper from archived data and fixed seeds, and checks fourteen anchor results.
  • DatasetPrefill and decode yields fitted to monthly benchmark snapshots for each model, hardware and latency floor, with each monthly change split into expected progress and shock. CC BY 4.0.
  • Pre-registrationFive hypotheses written before any settlement print exists, tested by a locked script against a locked document. Negative results get published too.

Glossary

Six terms, in plain words.

  • Prefill and decodeReading the prompt and writing the answer. They use the GPU differently and cost different amounts of its time.
  • YieldTokens per second one GPU produces while holding the latency floor. Furrow fits one yield for prefill and one for decode from public benchmarks.
  • Expected progress and shockThe average monthly change in a yield, which a planner can build in, and the surprises around it, which are risk.
  • BasisThe gap between what your provider charges and the market index. Known today, uncertain tomorrow, and not covered by a contract on the index.
  • Hedge effectivenessThe share of cost variance a policy removes compared with buying everything on demand. One hundred percent would mean a bill you know in advance.
  • Shapley shareThe fraction of variance attributed to one factor when every factor is uncertain at once, so the shares add up to the whole.
