Secrets as a Service

Soon, everything will need this. A way for a public blockchain to keep a secret and open it exactly on time, with nobody able to read it early and nobody able to hold it back.

Put a password in a smart contract and the whole world can read it.

Send a transaction and the whole network sees it before it is even included in a block. That transparency is the entire reason these systems can be trusted, because anyone can check them. But it quietly rules out anything that depends on timing.

A sealed bid that should stay hidden until the auction closes. A vote that should not show which way the wind is blowing while people are still voting. A security flaw that should go public on a fixed date whether or not the vendor cooperates. None of these work on a normal chain, because there is nowhere to put a secret that the chain itself will reveal later.

Software as a service let you rent computation instead of running it yourself. Secrets as a service is the same idea pointed at a stranger problem. You hand the network a secret and a deadline. It holds the secret, then opens it exactly when the deadline arrives, with nobody able to read it early and nobody able to hold it back.

Hand the network a secret and a deadline. It keeps both.
01

The idea: a letter that opens itself

Picture a sealed letter you drop into a box. You cannot take it back. Nobody can read it. And on a date written on the front it opens by itself, in public, and everyone can confirm it was never opened before. No trusted lawyer holding the envelope, no company you have to believe.

That is the whole product. You encrypt something, point it at a future moment, and the network opens it then, writes the result onto Ethereum, and attaches proof that it stayed shut until the deadline. The interesting question is the obvious one. How do you lock something to a time, with nobody holding the key?

02

How you lock something to a time

The answer is a handful of cryptographic pieces that a team called Commonware has been building and publishing. Each one is simple on its own. Stacked together, they do something that feels like it should not be possible.

A key split among many

Start with the lock. Instead of one party holding the decryption key, which means trusting that party, the key is split across a committee. Picture a safe whose key is divided among ten people, arranged so that any seven of them together can open it, but six or fewer learn nothing at all. Each member can produce a small piece of the answer, and enough pieces combine into the original message. This is called threshold encryption. The point is that you never trust any single person. You only trust that fewer than seven of the ten are dishonest.

Split the key across a committee. Enough members cooperating opens the secret, and too few learn nothing at all.

Turning time into the key

Now the clever part. We do not want the committee deciding when to open the safe. We want time to decide. So we tie the key to the clock.

The committee already produces a steady heartbeat. At each tick, a block or a round, it makes a joint signature over that tick's number, on a fixed schedule. It turns out a joint signature over a known, scheduled message can double as a decryption key. So if you know the committee's public key, and you know the message it is going to sign at round R, you can encrypt something today that only becomes openable once that signature exists, which is to say only at round R. Nobody can open it early, because the key literally does not exist yet. Nobody can stop it from opening, because the committee will sign round R no matter what. Time becomes the key.

The best part is that this already runs in the wild. A public service called drand, run by the League of Entropy, is exactly this kind of committee. It publishes a joint signature over a round number every few seconds, like a public clock anyone can build against. Timelock encryption to drand is a known, working recipe, so the first version of this can borrow drand as its clock and depend on nothing new.

Each round is signed on schedule. A secret aimed at round R opens only once that round's signature exists, and it always eventually does.

Making it un-cheatable

There is one gap left. Everything so far stops other people from reading your secret early, but on its own it does not stop you, the sender, from cheating. You could hand different sealed versions to different people, or quietly swap your envelope before it opens. This is where the blockchain earns its place. Store the encrypted blob in the chain's state, where it cannot be changed, and the cheating disappears. Once you have sealed, you cannot send a different version, and you cannot take it back. The chain provides the "you are committed" half. The cryptography provides the "locked to time" half. Together they are called binding timelock encryption.

Doing the hard homework early

One last idea, and it is the one that makes the whole thing practical. Opening these sealed items involves heavy math, the kind of cryptography that is slow on a phone, let alone on a blockchain. If you ever need to open thousands at once, the cost can balloon. Commonware's research cuts that down sharply, and it includes a genuinely neat move: almost all of the heavy work depends only on the sealed items, not on the key that unlocks them. So you can do that work in advance, while the timer is still running, and leave only a sliver for the unlock itself. In their measurements, opening a batch of about two thousand items took roughly thirteen seconds of work, but only about one second of it had to happen at the unlock.

Almost all the cost is front-loaded, so when the deadline hits, only about a second of work is left.

The cryptography in plain english

Threshold encryption. A secret is encrypted to a group, and any large-enough subset of the group can decrypt it together, while a smaller subset learns nothing.

Timelock encryption. The decryption key is a signature the committee will produce at a set time, so a secret can be opened only once that moment arrives, and never before.

drand. A live public service, run by the League of Entropy, that publishes a joint signature over a round number every few seconds, like a clock anyone can build against.

Binding timelock encryption. Timelock encryption with the ciphertext stored on-chain, so the sender cannot swap it or take it back once it is sealed.

03

Why this runs on Vara.eth

Here is the catch with doing this on Ethereum directly. A normal smart contract cannot run that kind of math. It can check a signature, but it cannot decrypt, it cannot do the heavy number crunching, and it certainly cannot run a multi-party protocol. Gas costs would make it absurd even if it were possible.

Vara.eth is built for exactly this gap. Your program is real Rust code that runs off the main chain, in a network of machines, with room to breathe, up to two gigabytes of memory per program and many programs running at once, and its results are anchored back onto Ethereum with no bridge in between. So the heavy decryption work finally has somewhere to live, while the final answer still lands on Ethereum where everything else can use it.

And because Vara.eth uses a reverse-gas model, where the program pays for its own execution, the people sealing and revealing never hold any crypto or pay a fee. They just click. The pieces line up cleanly: heavy off-chain compute for the cryptography, parallel programs for the front-loaded work, free transactions for the user, and Ethereum anchoring so the revealed result becomes public record.

04

How it fits together

There are five moving parts. A client encrypts the payload and submits it. The program, living on Vara.eth, stores the encrypted blob so it cannot be changed, keeps a running tamper-proof log of every seal and reveal, and does the verifying and decrypting. An unlock source provides the time-based signature. The executors are the machines that run the program and do the heavy lifting in parallel. The Router anchors everything onto Ethereum, so the revealed result becomes part of the public record.

The program holds your blob unchangeably and turns a time-based signature into a result anchored on Ethereum.

Where the unlock signal comes from

This is the one real decision, and there are two clean answers. The simple one, available today, is to use drand as the clock. You encrypt to a future drand round, the program holds your blob until then, and when drand publishes that round's signature, anyone can hand it to the program. The program checks the signature and uses it to open your blob. drand is the committee, and the service needs nothing special from Vara to make this work. This is the version to build first.

The neater one, if it turns out to be possible, is to use Vara's own validators as the committee, so there is no outside dependency at all. That hinges on a single detail: whether Vara's validators already produce a joint signature over each block under a fixed group key. If they do, the whole thing can run self-contained. If not, drand does the job fine. Worth checking, not worth blocking on.

Sealing and revealing

Sealing is quick. You encrypt to a round, send it in for free, and the program stores and logs it. When the round arrives and the clock publishes its signature, anyone can trigger the reveal. The program checks the signature, finishes the small remaining bit of work, writes the result on-chain, and adds the reveal to its log.

While a sealed item waits, the executors do the heavy work in advance, so the reveal itself is fast.

Because every seal and reveal is recorded in that log, the program can hand you a compact proof of the whole story: this exact blob was sealed at this block, aimed at this round, and opened at that block, with nothing touched in between. That timeline proof is the part a researcher, an auditor, or a court actually cares about. In code, the two actions are short:

// seal: lock a payload to a future round, submit for free
const blob = await tlock.encrypt(payload, { round: unlockRound });
await seal(blob, unlockRound);

// reveal: once the clock publishes that round, anyone can open it
const sig = await drand.signature(unlockRound);
await reveal(id, sig);
05

What is solid, and what is not yet

What is solid is the cryptography. Threshold encryption, timelock encryption, and the batching tricks are published research with working code, and drand has run in production for years. Vara.eth is live, with its programs, its large memory, its free transactions, and its Ethereum anchoring all documented and working.

What we still have to build is the program itself. The real open question is whether the heavy cryptography compiles down and runs cleanly inside Vara.eth's runtime, and runs identically on every machine. The network has to agree, so the math has to be perfectly deterministic. If running the full decryption inside the program turns out to be too heavy, there is a clean fallback that suits Vara.eth well: instead of the program decrypting, let anyone submit the answer plus a proof, and have the program just check it. Checking is far cheaper than decrypting.

A note on not over-building. The fancy batch machinery is the answer to opening thousands of items per block, which is a problem this service will not have on day one. The first version should open them one at a time using drand, which is simple and obviously correct. The heavy machinery is there for later, if the volume ever shows up. Building the cathedral before anyone arrives would be a mistake.

06

What you would build with it

The clearest use is responsible disclosure of security flaws. A researcher finds a serious bug, writes it up, seals it, and points it at the ninety-day deadline. It publishes itself on that date whether or not the vendor has fixed it. The vendor cannot bury it. The researcher cannot be pressured into leaking it early or sitting on it forever. There is no escrow service in the middle to trust, just the clock and a verifiable record. That is a real, unsolved headache in security, and it lands with exactly the people who poke hardest at this kind of claim.

That is the flagship, but the same primitive sits underneath a lot of things people have wanted and could not safely build:

  • Sealed-bid auctions. Every bid is encrypted and they all open together at the close, so nobody can snipe or shade a bid by watching the others.
  • Coercion-resistant voting. Ballots stay sealed until polls close and then reveal at once, so there is no running tally to follow and no way to prove your vote to a buyer beforehand.
  • Scheduled key handover. Hand over a credential, an API key, or access that only unlocks at a set time, with no custodian holding it in the meantime.
  • Embargoed releases. Press, earnings, or research locked to an embargo date, and provably untouched until that date arrives.
  • Dead man's switch. Files that release themselves if you stop checking in, with no third party ever holding the originals.
  • Front-running protection. Encrypt pending transactions so the ordering is locked before anyone can read them, the encrypted mempool that timelock research was built for.
For a live demo, the magic is the self-opening seal itself: a sealed message with a countdown, that the network cracks open at the deadline, with proof it stayed shut the whole way.
07

The one question that decides the rest

Almost everything here is settled. The cryptography exists, drand is live, and Vara.eth is running. The single fork in the road is whether Vara's validators already produce the kind of per-block joint signature that would let this run entirely on its own, without leaning on drand. That one answer decides whether secrets as a service is a drand-powered tool or a fully self-contained one. It is the first thing to nail down. Everything after that is building.
