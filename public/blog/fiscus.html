Fiscus: Private Asset Management on Miden

Every onchain asset manager today is transparent by force — positions, strategy, and every rebalance sit in public view for competitors to read. Fiscus is the first vault and asset-management protocol that keeps a manager's positions private while leaving solvency and compliance fully provable.

In Rome, the fiscus was the imperial treasury: kept separate from the public purse, yet still answerable to the state. Private and accountable at once. That is the one thing onchain capital has never been able to do.

Every onchain asset manager today is transparent by force. Their positions, their strategy, every rebalance sits in public view for competitors to read. The institutions now arriving will not run a book like that. Fiscus is the first vault and asset-management protocol that keeps a manager's positions private while leaving solvency and compliance fully provable. You cannot build that on a public chain. On Miden it falls out of the base layer.

01

The thesis: onchain asset management is growing up

Curated vaults already run into the billions, and traditional money has started paying attention. But the whole category lives on transparent chains. A fund's positions and strategy are visible to anyone who looks, competitors included, and they copy and front-run accordingly. For the kind of capital this space is trying to win, that is a non-starter.

Fiscus is a protocol for private vaults and asset management, built on Miden. Managers run strategies without exposing their positions. Allocators still get a provable share value. Auditors and regulators still get provable compliance. You cannot build that on a public chain — on Miden it falls out of the base layer.
02

The problem: transparent by force

Every major onchain asset manager, from Enzyme and dHEDGE to the curated vaults on Morpho and Veda, runs into the same wall: the chain is public, so the vault is glass. That creates three problems, and each one gets sharper as the capital gets larger.

i Copy-trading and front-running. When a manager's positions and trades are out in the open, competitors clone the alpha and bots front-run the rebalances. Transparency ends up punishing the managers who are actually good at this.

ii No room for institutions. No serious fund will run its book somewhere rivals can watch every move. The single biggest blocker to institutional capital coming onchain is that there has been nowhere private to put it.

iii Compliance is bolted on, not built in. Solvency, reserves, clean sources: today these are handled with off-chain attestations and a request to trust them, because a public chain cannot prove a private fact. The newer vault standards keep reaching for exactly this, which tells you the demand is real and the base layer is working against it.

Onchain asset management is still a rounding error next to the traditional industry, and that gap is the whole opportunity. The money is already moving in. Traditional managers are buying into curated-vault infrastructure and exchanges are routing deposits into onchain vaults. They will route a great deal more once they can do it privately.

03

Why now: three curves cross at the same moment

01 The money is arriving. Curated vaults turned onchain asset management from a curiosity into real infrastructure, and traditional finance has started treating it as legitimate. The next wave of capital is larger than the last and far more sensitive about privacy.

02 Miden hits mainnet. For the first time there is a production chain where private state, programmable accounts, and client-side proofs are native instead of bolted on afterwards. The foundation Fiscus is built on simply did not exist before now.

03 Privacy went from taboo to requirement. The conversation has shifted from privacy as hiding to privacy with provenance, which really means selective visibility. That shift is the thing institutional capital was holding out for, and it is the idea the whole protocol is built around.

04

The product: two things in one protocol

One place for managers to run private vaults, and one place for anyone to put capital into them. A clean, mobile-first app sits over the top, so the cryptography stays out of sight and allocating takes a single tap.

The three pillars

01 Private vaults, managed. A manager spins up a vault with a mandate: allowed assets, position and leverage limits, a fee schedule. Then they run their strategy. The positions stay private, and the mandate is enforced by the vault itself, not by trusting the manager to stay in line. Allocators deposit and hold private shares.

02 Private strategies, self-managed. A fund or an experienced user runs their own private portfolio on the same primitives. Nothing leaks, nothing gets copied, and they keep full programmable control.

03 The mobile platform. The whole thing lives in a phone app. Allocators find vaults and put money in; managers create and run them from the same screen. The privacy and proving sit underneath, where neither side has to think about them.

05

How it works: each piece maps to a Miden primitive

Fiscus is not a stack of shielded-pool contracts bolted onto a public chain. Every piece of it lands on a native Miden primitive, and that is what lets it be private and provable at the same time. Here is the whole system first, then each part on its own.

A vault is a private Miden account. Allocators deposit via notes, the manager trades client-side, and only commitments and proofs ever touch the chain.

Fiscus conceptMiden primitive
a vaulta private Miden account, holding assets and the strategy as account code
positions & sharesprivate account state; the chain stores only a commitment
deposits & redemptionsnotes, a request-then-claim async flow
the mandate (limits)programmable auth; the account inspects each trade and aborts if it breaks a limit
solvency & complianceclient-side ZK proofs over private state, with selective disclosure
pricing / NAVan oracle (Pragma) read via foreign procedure invocation
custody & recoverythe Guardian: non-custodial backup, sync, and threshold co-signing
settlement & authsettles to Ethereum via AggLayer; post-quantum Falcon auth, native

5.1 — One private account, four parts

A Fiscus vault is one Miden account running in private storage mode. Everything that would be a sprawl of separate contracts on another chain lives inside it: the assets, the share ledger, the positions, and the code that runs the strategy and enforces the rules. The network only ever sees a commitment to the whole account.

Nothing inside is public. The network stores a single commitment to the whole account.

5.2 — Deposit and redeem, as private notes

Capital moves in and out as notes, not public transfers. A deposit arrives as a request; the vault consumes it, prices it against the oracle, and answers by minting private shares. Redemptions run the same loop backwards. Because it works as request-then-claim, it absorbs the settlement delays real funds live with, and every step of it stays private.

Both legs settle as notes, so the share ledger updates without ever going public.

5.3 — A limit the manager cannot break

This is where the trust actually comes from. The manager runs the strategy but cannot step outside the mandate, because the limits live in the account's own auth code and execute inside every single transaction. Either the transaction proves it stayed within the rules, or it reverts in full. Allocators are not trusting the manager here. They are trusting the vault.

Every proposed trade passes through the vault's auth code. Within mandate it executes. Outside it, the transaction reverts.

miden · masm — vault mandate
use.miden::account
use.miden::tx

# the mandate, enforced inside the transaction itself. the manager signs
# the trade, but the account decides whether it is allowed, and reverts if not.
export.auth__mandate
    # 1. verify the manager's Falcon signature (native, post-quantum)
    # 2. introspect the transaction: which assets move, what size, where to
    exec.tx::get_output_notes_info
    # 3. assert within mandate (allowed assets, max position, leverage),
    #    and abort the WHOLE transaction if any limit is broken
    push.MAX_POSITION lte assert.err="mandate breach"
    exec.account::incr_nonce
end

5.4 — Valuing a private book

To mint shares and prove value, the vault needs prices it can rely on without giving away what it is pricing. It reads the Pragma oracle through foreign procedure invocation, works out net asset value inside the private account, and commits to the result. That NAV becomes a figure the vault can later prove to an allocator, with none of the underlying positions on show.

5.5 — What the app actually calls

All of that complexity sits behind a single surface, so the app and any integrator deal in plain verbs: allocate, position, create vault.

fiscus · ts — the facade
const fiscus = await Fiscus.connect({ endpoint });

// allocate into a vault: a one-tap PRIVATE deposit. the note is delivered
// off-band and the proof is generated on the device (or delegated).
await fiscus.allocate({ vault: vaultId, token: USDC, amount: 10_000n });

// your private position. only you, and whoever you disclose to, can see it.
const pos = await fiscus.position(vaultId);   // { shares, value, pnl }

// a manager creates a vault with the mandate baked into the account code
const vault = await fiscus.createVault({
  name: "Delta-Neutral USD",
  mandate: { assets: ["USDC", "ETH"], maxLeverage: 2 },
  fees: { management: 0.02, performance: 0.20 },
});
06

Selective disclosure: the killer feature

Privacy on its own is just a black box, and nobody hands real money to a black box. The actual product is not the hiding, it is proving without revealing. The vault keeps its holdings private, but it can prove any specific fact about them on demand, to whichever party has the right to ask.

Private from competitors. Provable to the people who matter. That is the whole unlock.

Three kinds of proof cover most of what allocators and regulators ask for in practice. A scoped view key handles whatever falls outside them.

01 Proof of value. NAV, and the value of an allocator's own shares, so people are trusting math rather than a screenshot.

02 Proof of solvency. Proof that assets cover liabilities, without putting either figure on the table.

03 Proof of provenance. Proof that every incoming asset came from an approved set, an association-set or proof-of-innocence style check, run inside the note logic.

fiscus · ts — selective disclosure
// prove the truth about private state without revealing it.
const solvency = await vault.prove("assets_ge_liabilities");
const nav      = await vault.prove("nav", { asOf: block });
const clean    = await vault.prove("sources_in_approved_set");

// for anything deeper, grant a scoped VIEW KEY: read-only, time-boxed,
// revocable, decrypting only what that party is cleared to see.
const auditorKey = await vault.grantViewKey(auditor, {
  scope: "positions", until: quarterEnd,
});
A transparent vault shows everyone everything, which is too much. A naively private vault shows no one anything, which is too little. Fiscus sits in between on purpose: it discloses a given fact to the party entitled to it, and proves the fact is true. That middle ground is the selective-visibility model Miden was designed around.
07

The mobile platform: the phone is where the privacy lives

Miden is an edge chain: execution and proving both happen on the user's own device. That makes it an unusually good fit for a real mobile product. The phone here is not a thin client pointed at a server, it is where the privacy is generated in the first place.

01 Proving on the device, delegated when needed. Light actions prove right on the phone. Heavier strategy proofs get delegated to a prover so the app never stalls, and the SDK makes that call on its own, so the user never has to think about it.

02 Both sides, same screen. Allocators get discovery, one-tap allocation, and a private portfolio view with values they can prove. Managers get vault creation, mandate setup, and strategy controls. Same product, showing each side what it needs.

03 Privacy is invisible. The hardest lesson from a decade of private payments is that privacy dies at the UX layer. Real people will not manage shielded addresses. Fiscus buries all of that: putting money into a vault feels about as involved as sending a message.

The competition lives in desktop dapps, because on a public chain the phone does not buy you anything extra. On Miden the phone is the execution and privacy layer, so a polished mobile app stops being a nicety. It becomes a structural edge that nobody on a transparent chain can reproduce.

1 Crypto-native funds & DAOs. They feel the copy / front-run pain most, onboard fastest, and bring their own allocators.

2 Pro retail allocators. One-tap allocate into vetted private vaults, reached via the managers' audiences and the app.

3 Institutions. Private positions + provable compliance + Guardian custody + auditor access.

Every manager Fiscus signs brings their own allocators along, so growth compounds from the manager side of the market. The positioning is short and it sticks: the private Enzyme, where serious capital manages money onchain without showing its hand.

11

Risks and honest limits: naming the hard parts

A serious project says its hard parts out loud. None of the following is fatal. Pretending none of it existed would be.

On Miden's current testnet the node can re-execute transactions, which means users have privacy from each other but not yet from the node operator. Fiscus is built around that fact and hardens alongside the network on the way to mainnet. It will never claim more privacy than the chain can actually back up.

i Privacy vs trust. The DeFi instinct is that transparency is what builds trust, so a private vault sounds dangerous at first. The answer is selective disclosure. Fiscus can prove more than a transparent vault can — solvency and compliance and provenance — whenever someone asks. It is not a black box. It is a glass box, and you decide where the windows go.

ii Regulation. Private finance attracts regulators, and it should. Fiscus treats compliance as a first-class feature rather than an afterthought. Provable clean sources and auditor access are built in from the start, which turns the regulatory question from a liability into a selling point.

iii Pricing and NAV. Pricing a private portfolio takes reliable oracles and conservative accounting. Fiscus leans on a vetted oracle and proves the NAV instead of just asserting it.

iv Account-code and cold-start risk. Mandate logic has to be audited and formally checked, and a two-sided market is genuinely hard to start, which is the whole reason the wedge leads with managers who arrive with their own allocators.
