01 · The problem

Poker, cards face up

Blockchains earned their reputation by making everything visible. For payments and public markets, that transparency is the feature. For a fund, it is the fatal flaw. A fund's product is its judgement: which assets to hold, when to move, what everyone else has missed. Publish that in real time and you have given the product away for free, to the exact people paid to use it against you.

  • i Your homework gets copied

    Any wallet on earth can watch a skilled manager's positions and mirror them a block later, paying none of the research cost. Being good becomes a subsidy for being fast.

  • ii The market sees your order on its way to the till

    Bots read a pending rebalance and trade ahead of it, so the fund buys a little higher and sells a little lower, every single time. Death by a thousand basis points.

  • iii Serious money simply stays away

    No real institution will run a book where rivals watch every move live. Ask why trillions in traditional assets have not come onchain and this is a large part of the honest answer: there has been nowhere private to put them.

And yet the money keeps arriving

Onchain fund products already manage billions through curated vaults, and traditional firms have started buying the infrastructure behind them. All of that has happened despite the glass walls. The open question, and the whole Fiscus bet, is how much more arrives once the walls can be turned opaque without anyone having to take honesty on faith.

02 · What Fiscus is

One paragraph, then three people

Fiscus is a protocol for private investment funds, built on a blockchain called Miden. A manager opens a vault, sets its rules, and trades. The positions stay sealed. Investors put money in and hold private shares. And instead of trusting anyone, every party gets mathematical proof of the one thing they actually need to know. That is the whole product. Everything else in this guide is how it works and why it wins.

The cleanest way to understand it is through the three people who use it. They will reappear throughout this guide.

03 · The idea

The glass box you control the windows of

Here is the mental model that carries everything else. Today's onchain funds are glass boxes: everyone sees everything, which is too much. An old-fashioned private fund is a steel box: nobody sees anything, which is too little, because you cannot hand real money to a mystery. Fiscus is the third option, and it only recently became possible to build.

A private pool of wealth that could not simply be taken on faith. Two thousand years later, onchain finance still has not managed to build one. That is the gap.

04 · The machine underneath

Meet Miden, the chain that checks receipts

Fiscus is only possible because of how Miden works, so two minutes here pays for itself. Most blockchains operate like a courtroom where every juror re-does your taxes to confirm you got them right. Effective, but slow, and your whole return is read aloud. Miden flips it: you do your own taxes on your own device, and you hand the network a cryptographic receipt so airtight that checking the seal is as good as re-doing the work. Your numbers never leave your hands. Three building blocks matter.

  • 01 Accounts are safes with the rules built into the lock

    Every Miden account carries its own code. A Fiscus vault is one private account holding the assets, the investor ledger, and the rules, and the chain stores only a fingerprint of it. Change anything inside and the fingerprint changes, so nothing can be quietly rewritten.

  • 02 Notes are certified envelopes

    Money and messages move as notes: sealed envelopes only the named recipient can open, where the opening itself is a verifiable act. A deposit is an envelope to the vault. Shares come back the same way. Nothing crosses a public counter.

  • 03 Proofs are receipts that cannot lie

    Because every action produces one of those airtight receipts, an account can prove a fact about its hidden contents, the fund is solvent, say, without opening the safe. This one trick is the entire Fiscus product, applied over and over.

05 · A day in the life

Maya opens a vault. Alex invests.

Theory over. Let's just watch it happen. Maya runs a market-neutral crypto strategy that has been quietly bleeding returns to copy-traders for a year. She opens a Fiscus vault from her phone in about the time it takes to make coffee: pick the allowed assets, set position and leverage limits, set her fees, choose how often the fund settles. Those choices become the mandate, and here is the part that matters: the mandate is not a promise she makes. It is written into the vault's own lock, and from this moment it binds her too.

5.1Alex puts money in

Alex finds Maya's vault in the app: her provable track record, her rules, her fees, nothing else, because nothing else is anyone's business. He taps to invest. Under the hood his deposit becomes a sealed envelope addressed to the vault, and it waits there with everyone else's. A fund is not a vending machine, so Fiscus does not pretend to be one. Instead the vault runs on a settlement rhythm, like a fund's end-of-day bell.

Alex's money, step by step
everyone who queues in a cycle gets the same fair price · nobody can jump the line
request
Alex's deposit arrives as a sealed envelope. It sits in the queue, cancelable, not yet priced.
the bell
The cycle closes. The vault checks live prices, values itself, and fills the whole queue at one price.
shares issued
An envelope comes back holding Alex's private shares, and the valuation is pinned to a public registry.
owned
Alex opens it. The shares now live in his own sealed account. Cashing out runs the same loop in reverse.

Because the price only comes into existence at the bell, there is nothing to front-run and no way to game the entry.

5.2Maya trades, inside the box she drew

Now Maya works. She proposes a trade from her device, signs it, and the trade goes through only after her own vault has inspected it. Every trade, every time, no exceptions and no override key. Watch what happens when she pushes the limit.

Alex is not trusting Maya's discipline. He is trusting a lock that inspects the bag on the way out.

5.3And the world gets its one honest number

At each bell the vault also publishes a checkpoint: the fund's value per share, stamped with the prices it used, pinned to a public registry the vault can add to but never edit. Alex's app reads it to show him what he owns. Maya's next investor reads the whole chain of checkpoints as a track record no one can fake. And if the market data ever looks wrong, jumping further than the vault's safety bound allows, the vault refuses to publish and freezes instead. A manipulated price gets you a stuck fund, not a mispriced one. Boring is the point.

06 · The rules

Nobody here trusts anybody, and it works

The deepest habit Fiscus borrows from the best vault systems on public chains is this: never let one person, or one stolen key, be enough to hurt anyone. Power in a Fiscus vault is split four ways, and each piece is enforced by the vault itself rather than by anyone's good behavior.

  • 01 The curator draws the box

    Sets the mandate: assets, limits, fees, settlement rhythm. Usually an institution's committee holding a multi-person key. Wants to loosen a rule? The change waits out a public delay first.

  • 02 The manager trades inside it

    Maya's key can invoke trades and nothing else. It cannot touch the rules, the fees, or anyone's money on the way out. Steal her key and all you can do is trade competently inside her own limits.

  • 03 The sentinel is the brake pedal

    Can pause deposits, cut limits, force the exit queue to settle, and veto a pending rule change. Every power points the same direction: down, toward less risk. Brakes work instantly, because a brake on a delay is a decoration.

  • 04 Investors hold the exits

    Alex's right to leave at fair value is untouchable by the other three. And when the curator proposes a looser rule, that public delay exists precisely so Alex can read it and walk out before it takes effect.

6.1What the lock actually checks

For the technically curious, here is the door check in plain English, laid out the way the vault's code runs it. Everyone else can skim the shape and move on: the point is that it runs last, on every transaction, against what the transaction really did rather than what anyone claimed.

the door check · runs last, every transaction
# 1 · who signed this?
identify → manager | curator | sentinel

# 2 · what actually moved? (the vault's own tally, not the claim)
inspect → every asset out, every rule touched

# 3 · manager? then every movement must fit the mandate
require asset ∈ allowlist
require position ≤ cap        # each one, and all together
require leverage ≤ limit
require rules untouched       # she cannot redraw her own box

# 4 · curator? rule changes only through a matured delay
# 5 · sentinel? only moves that reduce risk

any check fails → the whole transaction never existed
Why a lock beats a promise

On public chains, rules usually live in a wrapper around the vault, and the sharpest failures in DeFi history were people finding a way around the wrapper. Here the check runs inside the same proof that makes the transaction real, after the chain's own accounting of what moved. There is no around. Breaking the mandate is not forbidden so much as impossible to express.

07 · Proving without showing

The noticeboard and the visitor badge

Everything so far protects the fund. This section is what protects everyone else, and it is the part competitors on transparent chains cannot copy, because it needs a fund that can prove facts about contents nobody can see.

The anchor is the registry: a public noticeboard where every vault pins its settlement checkpoints. Pinned means pinned. The vault can add the next one but can never edit or remove an old one, so its history is held outside its own hands. Every proof Fiscus produces points back at a pin on that board, which is why a stranger can verify Maya's claims without ever contacting Maya. Five proofs cover nearly everything anyone asks a fund.

  • 01 What is it worth?

    The fund's value per share, and Alex's own stake, so his balance is arithmetic rather than a screenshot he chooses to believe.

  • 02 Can it pay everyone back?

    Assets cover what is owed, proven without revealing either number. The question behind every fund collapse in history, answered before it is asked.

  • 03 Is the money clean?

    Every deposit checked against approved sources on arrival, at the door, not reconstructed in a panic later.

  • 04 Did it follow its own rules?

    A whole settlement cycle proven to have stayed inside the mandate. A fund's riskiness stops being a claim and becomes a checkable object.

  • 05 Is the track record real?

    The chain of checkpoints proves performance across time with zero positions revealed. For Maya, this one is a business asset, and section 10 is about why.

For the rare case where someone is legally owed a deeper look, the auditor at quarter-end, a regulator with a warrant, there is the visitor badge: a view key scoped to one wing of the vault, expiring on a date, revocable early. Granting one is itself a logged act. Deeper access happens on the fund's terms, in the open, instead of through a leak.

08 · The credit line

fUSD, or how sealed shares stay spendable

A stake in a good fund has the same problem as a good house: it is worth a lot and you cannot spend a bedroom. Selling means leaving returns on the table, and fund shares settle on a rhythm anyway. The traditional answer is a mortgage, borrow against the house and keep living in it. fUSD is a mortgage against Fiscus vault shares, with one difference no bank can offer: the lender never learns your address.

8.1How Alex uses it

Alex's stake in Maya's vault has grown and he needs cash for something else. Instead of cashing out, he locks his shares in a private position of his own and mints fUSD, a dollar-steady token, against a safe fraction of their proven value. The value comes from Maya's public checkpoints, so the system can price his collateral without ever seeing it. His debt, his position, even the fact that he borrowed: sealed. What the world sees is only the totals: how much fUSD exists and that the whole system holds more collateral than debt, proven fresh every cycle. One green number standing on thousands of sealed positions.

8.2The mountain-climber rule

Here is the genuinely new problem. On a transparent chain, risky loans get handled because everyone can see a position drowning and someone steps in. Behind sealed walls, nobody can see anything, so Fiscus flips the duty around, the way mountain rescue does. A climber who stops radioing base camp is presumed in trouble. Alex's position must periodically check in with a fresh proof that it is still healthy against the latest prices. Miss the check-in and a timer note matures that anyone may trigger for a small reward, flagging the position. A short grace window lets Alex top up or repay. If he does not, the system settles it, and even that stays civilized.

8.3The tow truck is paid for in advance

Fiscus keeps a rescue pool, filled ahead of time by people who deposit fUSD to earn the borrowing fees. When a position fails, the pool absorbs the debt on the spot and receives a claim on the underlying shares at a discount, settled through the vault's ordinary exit queue at the next bell. No fire-sale auction, no identity revealed, no waiting for a buyer during a crash. The best transparent-chain lenders proved that a pre-funded rescuer is the fastest honest way to handle failure. Behind sealed walls it is the only honest way.

Every state change is stamped on the public ledger, so the system is auditable even though every position inside it is sealed.

8.4Kept deliberately boring

fUSD is over-collateralized debt and nothing cleverer, because a stable token's job description is to be dull. Borrowers set their own small ongoing fee, and when holders redeem fUSD for collateral at face value, the redemptions hit the lowest-fee positions first, so every borrower prices their own risk of being tapped. Each vault gets a ceiling on how much fUSD its shares may back, so one fund failing is a bad day, never a system event. And if the whole system's cushion ever thins past its floor, new borrowing halts everywhere until repayments and rescues rebuild it. There is no algorithmic anything. The graveyard is full of clever stablecoins.

Why the fund comes first and the credit line comes second

fUSD leans on everything beneath it: honest checkpoints, sturdy price feeds, a registry with history, and real funds whose shares are worth borrowing against. So it ships after the fund platform has weight on it, not beside it. That ordering shows up again in the roadmap, and it is a dependency, not a hedge.

09 · The business

Paid the way funds have always paid

Asset management has run on the same commercial engine for a century: managers charge fees on the money they run, and the platform takes a slice. Fiscus keeps that engine and simply becomes the best place to run it. No advertising, no selling user data, obviously, and no dependence on trading volume. Revenue grows when the funds grow, which keeps the protocol pointed the same direction as its users.

Where money comes fromIn plain terms
Slice of management feesMaya charges her investors a yearly percentage; the protocol takes a small cut of that fee, not of Alex's principal
Slice of performance feeswhen Maya beats her own high-water mark she earns a share of profits, and the protocol takes a sliver of it, so the protocol only wins when investors already have
Vault creation & listinga small fee to open and list a vault, mostly there to keep spam out of discovery
The institutional tierthe premium layer serious money requires: auditor badge portals, custody integration with co-signing, compliance reporting. Hard to build, harder to copy, priced accordingly
fUSD borrowing feesonce the credit line ships, borrowers' ongoing fees flow to the rescue pool and the protocol, a second engine that scales with the first

Two structural notes worth making. Fees on assets compound: a platform holding sticky funds earns in bear markets too, unlike anything paid per transaction. And a token is deliberately not part of the model at the start. One can come later for governance and aligning managers with investors, but the protocol earns real revenue from the first vault onward, token or no token. Businesses that need a token to have a business are not businesses yet.

10 · How it grows

Win the people who are bleeding

A fund platform is a two-sided market, managers on one side and investors on the other, and two-sided markets die of cold starts. The way through is to begin with the people whose pain is sharpest, because they move first and they bring the other side with them.

1 Crypto-native funds & DAOs
the Mayas: visibly losing returns to copy-traders today, fastest to adopt, and each one arrives with her own investors
2 Pro-am allocators
the Alexes: one tap into vetted private funds with provable value, reached through the managers' own audiences and the app
3 Institutions
sealed positions plus provable compliance plus real custody: the checklist that has kept them out, finally checkable

The engine hiding inside stage one is the track record. Recall proof number five: Maya can now show any allocator on earth mathematically verified returns without leaking a single position. That has never existed before. Every pitch she makes anywhere carries Fiscus with it, a portable badge that only works because the platform underneath is real. Managers recruit investors, verified records recruit managers, and the flywheel needs no ad budget.

11 · What could go wrong

Said plainly, on purpose

A guide that hides the hard parts is an advertisement. Here are the real ones, each with what actually happens rather than what marketing would prefer. The pattern to notice: the system is built so that failures cost money, not the system, and never quietly.

If this happensWhat actually follows
Maya goes rogueshe can trade badly inside her mandate, and that is all. She cannot withdraw Alex's money, exceed her limits, loosen her own rules, or block the exits. Bad judgement stays possible; theft stops being expressible
A curator key is stolenthe thief can propose a looser rule, which then sits in a public delay while sentinels veto it and investors walk out. Multi-person keys make the theft itself unlikely; the delay makes it unprofitable
A price feed liesfeeds are blended across publishers, and a valuation that jumps past its safety bound freezes the vault instead of repricing it. Manipulation buys a halt, not a heist
A fund collapses with fUSD against itper-vault ceilings cap the damage, the rescue pool absorbs the debt, and the loss lands on that pool's willing depositors. Painful, contained, never a run on the token
A borrower disappearsthe missed check-in flags the position, grace expires, the pool settles it. Nobody needed to find them, and nobody learned who they were
The network operator peeks, todaythe honest one. On the current testnet the operator could re-execute transactions, so privacy holds against other users but not yet against the operator. That gap closes on the road to mainnet, and Fiscus will not advertise privacy the chain cannot yet deliver

Two quieter risks belong on the record too. Heavy strategy proofs can be delegated to a proving service for speed, and whoever proves sees what they prove, so sensitive vaults keep proving on their own devices and the trade-off stays a named choice rather than a default. And the rules engine itself, the lock everything relies on, gets the treatment locks deserve: external audits before every launch phase, formal checks on the mandate code, and launch caps that widen only as the record accumulates.

12 · The road, and who is on it

Three phases, one dependency graph

The order below is not a wish list, it is the order the pieces lean on each other. The vault has to work before anyone should manage money in it. Money has to be managed in it before shares are worth borrowing against. Each phase ends with a test you can check, not a vibe.

Phase 1 · prove the loop

One vault, end to end, on testnet

One real manager runs full cycles: create with a mandate, take deposits, trade, settle, publish checkpoints. Done means a stranger's laptop can verify the fund's claims with no help from us.

Phase 2 · open the platform

Managers, the app, the badges

Many vaults, the mobile app for both sides, fees with high-water marks, auditor badges, custody with co-signing, and the first outside manager cohort. Done means a fund operating with zero Fiscus-team keys anywhere near it.

Phase 3 · mainnet & the credit line

Real stakes, capped then widened

Mainnet behind conservative caps, then fUSD: the issuer, the rescue pool, the check-in clock, the public solvency number. Done means the first failed position handled entirely by the pool, with nobody doxxed and nothing owed.
