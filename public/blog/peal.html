Programmable Disclosure · Batched Threshold Encryption

Peal: encryption with a release date

Seal a payload today. It stays unreadable, even to the servers holding it, until a time or block you set. Then the network itself decrypts it, in the open, whether you come back or not. Peal is a thin coordination layer over Commonware's batched threshold encryption; this is how it works and what it unlocks.

Most ways to hide data on a public chain hide it with a hash and ask the user to reveal the real value later. Peal encrypts it instead, and hands the reveal to a committee that is obligated to open it on cue. The result behaves like a sealed envelope with a printed open-by date that opens itself.

01 · The problem

Reveal is a step the loser can skip

Commit-reveal is the standard trick: post a hash of your secret now, post the secret later. It works only if people come back, and the people who lose have every reason not to.

  • Sealed-bid auctions cannot tell a griefing bidder from a crashed one. The loser never opens their bid.
  • On-chain votes stall when a voter who saw the early tally move decides silence is better.
  • Treasury trades leak the moment intent hits a governance forum, and the market reprices before a unit moves.

Every app built on voluntary reveal pays the same tax: reveal windows, timeout knobs, slashing deposits sized to a guess, incomplete data, and a support queue of honest users who missed their window. The root cause is structural. If revealing is a user action, users decline it exactly when declining pays.

02 · The idea

Make the reveal a network duty

Peal removes the second step. You encrypt the payload in your browser and submit it once. A cue you choose, a wall-clock time or a block height, freezes a batch of these ciphertexts. A committee of operators then decrypts the whole batch in public. Before the cue, nobody can read anything, and that includes the coordinator that stores the data. After it, everyone can. Your only action was the seal.

03 · How it works

A committee, a batch, one share each

Five operators hold key material. Any three can open a batch; fewer than three can open nothing. Each operator keeps its keys locally and only ever calls outward to the coordinator, so no single machine, the coordinator included, can decrypt on its own. The coordinator is a scheduler and aggregator, not a key holder.

A payload moves through five stages:

  1. 01 Seal in the browser

    Wasm runs the encryption locally. Only the sealed bytes leave the device; the plaintext never exists outside the tab that typed it.

  2. 02 Submit to a condition

    The coordinator stores the blob under its cue and content-addresses it by hash. That hash is the stable handle your app keeps.

  3. 03 The cue fires, the batch freezes

    A 500 ms tick loop watches every condition. When one fires, the coordinator pads to 64 slots, sorts ciphertexts by hash, and locks positions. The frozen layout is deterministic: anyone with the same seals rebuilds the same board.

  4. 04 Operators post shares

    Each node computes one 48-byte partial for the entire batch and posts it. The coordinator verifies every share against public keys before counting it, so a forged share is caught and attributed to its operator, not trusted.

  5. 05 The batch reveals

    Any three valid shares open every slot at once. The reveal ships with a merkle root over all slots, so anyone can download the batch and recompute it. If fewer than three shares arrive within 120 s the condition stalls honestly, and one late valid share still completes it.

04 · Inside the cryptography

How Peal uses Commonware's BTE

Peal does not invent cryptography. The primitive underneath is Commonware's batched threshold encryption (eprint 2026/760, implemented in simple-bte), used unmodified. Everything that turns a scheme into a network, conditions, transport, verification, persistence, is the wrapper.

The punctured setup

The whole scheme turns on one idea. The setup publishes powers of a secret τ up to 2B, with exactly one power, τB+1, left out. Everyone can encrypt toward that missing power; nobody holds it. A single ciphertext cannot be climbed from a published power to the missing one, so it cannot be decrypted alone. The batch structure is what routes around the hole: pairing each ciphertext against the right published powers produces cross-terms in which the missing power appears only multiplied by the operators' secret-shared low powers. Combine t operators and it cancels; with fewer than t it stays hidden behind Shamir secrecy.

published
[τj] for j = 0 … 2B, except j = B+1 (zeroed)
encrypt toward
ek = [τB+1]ₜ, the one power never published
dealt to operators
Shamir shares of τ¹ … τB, threshold t of n

This is also why a batch is always exactly 64 slots: the setup material and FFT domain are sized to B, so short batches are padded with self-sealed dummies rather than shrinking the math.

The seal, and why tampering is caught

A sealed ciphertext is a 48-byte KEM header plus a masked key and body, 64 bytes of overhead total. The last line below is the Fujisaki-Okamoto discipline: the ephemeral scalar is not stored, it is re-derived from the recovered plaintext at decrypt time. A ciphertext anyone tampered with recovers to a plaintext whose re-derived scalar no longer reproduces the header, so that slot is flagged corrupt and quarantined while the other 63 open cleanly.

KEM header
ct₀ = [k]₁, 48 bytes compressed G1
masked key + body
ct₁ = Hₖ([k·τB+1]ₜ) xor K  ·  ct₂ = Hₘ(K) xor payload
integrity check
k = Hᵣ(K, payload), verify [k]₁ = ct₀

Shares and public verification

To open a batch, operator j runs a single multi-scalar multiplication over all 64 frozen headers and emits one compressed G1 point, 48 bytes, no matter how full the batch is. Before that share counts, the coordinator checks it against public verification keys with a pairing equation. Nothing secret is needed to verify, so anyone can re-run the check; a forged share simply fails it and is logged under the operator's identity.

operator's share
pdⱼ = Σᵢ σⱼᵢ · ctᵢ,₀
public check
e(pdⱼ, g₂) = Πᵢ e(ctᵢ,₀, vⱼᵢ)
recover
Lagrange-combine any t valid shares at x = 0

Recovery is pipelined

The expensive half of decryption never waits for operators. The FFT cross-terms cost O(B log B) group operations and O(B) pairings, not a naive B² loop, and they depend only on the frozen ciphertexts, so the coordinator computes them at freeze time while shares are still in flight. When three verified shares land, finalize is one Lagrange interpolation at x = 0 plus the per-slot integrity re-check. On the devnet that is roughly 250 ms of pre-decrypt and 40 to 150 ms of finalize, so the user-visible gap between cue and plaintext is the share round-trip, not the math.

The efficiency in one line

One constant-size share per operator opens up to 64 payloads at once, so committee cost per reveal stays flat as a batch fills, and every share is a public equation anyone can check.

The reveal is bound by a merkle root over (position, payload) for all 64 slots, padding included, and that root can be anchored onchain. A published reveal cannot be edited without breaking the root, and anyone can download the batch and recompute it.

05 · Use cases

What you can build

The filter that matters is the moat test: could plain commit-reveal do this? If it could, someone will do it cheaper. The five below only work when reveal is guaranteed by a committee the user does not control.

  1. 01 Verifiable AI agent track records

    An agent seals each prediction before the event, cue set to resolution time. When it fires, the whole batch opens, wins and losses together, so a record cannot be cherry-picked. Complete, unfakeable track records are the missing trust layer of the agent economy.

    why peal under commit-reveal the agent reveals its own calls and buries the losers; completeness exists only when it cannot choose.

  2. 02 Sealed-bid auctions and fair launches

    Token sales, NFT drops, and domain auctions where every bid stays sealed until close, then opens at one instant and one clearing price (uniform, or Vickrey where truthful bidding is optimal). No sniping, no whale wait-and-outbid, no gas war.

    why peal ENS tried sealed-bid on commit-reveal in 2017 and got locked funds and forgotten reveals; guaranteed reveal removes the second transaction and strategic non-reveal.

  3. 03 Sealed treasury execution

    A DAO commits a large clip under a cue; qualified market makers seal competing bids; the batch opens at once, clears at one price, and settles atomically against the reveal root, with a full audit trail after. Discretion before, transparency after.

    why peal the announcement is the leak, and guaranteed reveal stops bidders fishing with quotes they plan to abandon. No OTC desk offers that pairing.

  4. 04 On-chain voting

    Ballots stay secret until the poll closes, then all reveal together. No reveal window to police, no voter swayed by a partial early tally, no abandoned ballots skewing the count.

    why peal a voluntary reveal lets whoever dislikes the running result simply not open, which is the exact failure a committee removes.

  5. 05 Encrypted mempools and fair ordering

    Transactions are sealed until after they are ordered, then the batch reveals for execution. The front-running window closes because the intent a searcher would trade against does not exist until ordering is fixed.

    why peal MEV feeds on reading intent early; a batch that only opens post-ordering starves it of the signal.

One capability sits underneath all of these. The threshold reveal is public by design, which is exactly what auctions and votes need. For personal data, Peal adds a second client-side layer: the browser encrypts the payload again with a key that lives only in the share link's fragment and never touches a server. The network still proves when it opened; only the link holder learns what it said.

06 · Building on it

Four calls, no reveal to chase

Create a cue, seal locally, keep the returned hash, wait for the reveal. There is no second transaction to build.

app.ts · sealed-bid auction
import { BteClient } from 'bte-sdk';
const peal = new BteClient({ url: 'https://peal.network' });

// a cue 60 seconds out, tagged so this app finds its own rounds
const conditionId = await peal.condition({ in: 60, tag: 'auction-v1' });

// seal locally; bind app context inside the encrypted bytes
const sealed = await peal.seal(JSON.stringify({
  conditionId, bid: 42, nonce: crypto.randomUUID()
}), conditionId);

// the network does the rest
const reveal = await peal.waitForReveal(conditionId);
const slot = reveal.slots.find((s) => s.ctHash === sealed.ctHash);
console.log(slot.valid ? slot.text : 'did not recover');

Two habits keep integrations safe:

  • Bind the payload. Put the condition id, app name, and a nonce inside the encrypted bytes so a copied ciphertext cannot be replayed into another context.
  • Pin the committee. Ship the expected parameter digest with your app instead of accepting whatever committee the coordinator names.
